I play music for people.
Whether you love jazz, are hearing it for the first time, or have listened for a lifetime, it makes no difference to me.
Moving between Seoul, New York and Europe, I have played drums for about thirty years, and more than twenty of them have been spent with jazz. I have played stages big and small in some hundred cities across thirty-five countries, sharing music with all kinds of people.
I think jazz is not music you play well but music you listen to well. Before deciding what to play, listen to what the music is telling you right now, and find your place inside it.
I still believe one of the best things a drummer can do is wait until the music arrives on its own.

About

Drums first. Jazz came later.

Until my mid-twenties I did not like jazz. I liked drums. I was born in Daejeon, started on piano at five, and switched to drums the day I saw a drummer on television. At Kyunghee University I played everything except jazz, until a German bassist, Martin Zenker, dragged me to a club with the promise of a beer and called me up to play one tune.

A few years later I stood on a televised stage with New York saxophonist Jesse Davis, no rehearsal, no set list, and asked him about the tempo. He said: play what you hear. That night became the album Soul Searchin': Live in Seoul, and the start of twenty years of stages across New York, Europe and Asia.

Today I lead my own projects between Seoul and New York, write music between tours, and play for one reason I call my business secret: people come to feel good, and my job is to deliver.

    Lessons

    I don't teach licks first.

    Most jazz education starts with information: play this here, practice it like that. The information is correct and the music still does not happen. I teach it the other way round.

    1

    Play what you hear

    Before patterns, before vocabulary, you learn to hear the band and answer it. Deciding the tune in advance already puts an opinion in your head. We practice removing it.

    2

    Culture before information

    Jazz is how a people lived, not a chord chart. We listen to the records, talk about the lives behind the sound, and only then open the drum book.

    3

    Nobody gives orders

    On a good bandstand no one explains how to play. Direction comes from the music, feedback comes only when the direction is wrong, and the rest is trust. My lessons work the same way.

    4

    Take what I have that you don't

    I learned this from a professor who was behind me in jazz and far ahead in Latin and pop. You bring your strengths, I bring mine, and the lesson is a conversation.
